# An Empirical Model for the Analysis of Social Values of Student Youth

## Abstract

**Introduction.** The topic of values in general, as well as the problem of their study in particular, is gaining increasing popularity in modern Russian discourse, which is explained by the significant acceleration of social changes mediated by geopolitical and domestic political processes, as well as by the updated framework of legal regulation: strengthening the integration into public life of traditional Russian values, enshrined by Decree of the President of the Russian Federation of 09.11.2022 No. 809 "On the Approval of the Fundamentals of State Policy for the Preservation and Strengthening of Traditional Russian Spiritual and Moral Values." This state of affairs suggests the need for a sociological diagnosis of the value space of modern Russian society.

**Aim.** Today there are many tools for studying values, but most of them are aimed at assessing individual values, while we are interested in social values, which determined the purpose of the article: to develop and describe an empirical model for analyzing social values, which is based on the idea of the need to use a set of indicators for their cumulative assessment.

**Methods.** The indicators of the assessment of social values identified by the author include: value relations, value preferences, value attitudes, value actions. On the basis of the proposed approach, a sociological toolkit has been developed, which has been tested in the course of diagnosing the value space of student youth. The survey was conducted from May 17 to June 1, 2023 (N=4338).

**Results.** The results of testing the empirical model of the analysis of social values demonstrated a pronounced disparity in the respondents' attitude to the proposed indicators of their measurement, which is explained by the presence of a noticeable gap between the understanding of the ideal idea of values and actual adherence to them.

**Conclusions.** The tested empirical model of the analysis of social values can form the basis for studying social values not only of other socio-demographic groups, but also of Russian society as a whole.

social values, youth, sociology of values, sociology of youth, traditional values, higher education

## Introduction

The social nature of values, which are organically interwoven into all social processes, being both their regulators and their reference points, explains their integration into all spheres of the life of society. Values, in the words of M. A. Rozov, are certain "social patterns" that surround a person from the first to the last of their days; they are "a certain 'social field' that determines a person's acts, activity, and attitude to the world" [1, p. 7].

In a rapidly changing world, amid globalization and digitalization, an understanding of social values is becoming increasingly important for solving complex social, economic, and political problems. In this regard, the need to develop effective tools for analyzing the space of values, aimed at obtaining the information necessary for making well-grounded decisions at all levels of the organization of social life, is becoming increasingly evident. In addition, the relevance of developing such a toolkit is also explained by the fact that today an increasing orientation toward traditional Russian spiritual and moral values, enshrined in several regulatory legal acts at once,[^1] is coming to the fore in the formation of modern Russian society. This approach assumes that these values can not only be shared by the majority of the citizens of the Russian Federation, but also be certain universals transmitted from generation to generation, which determined the author's choice of them as the basis for further research.

However, despite the evident high relevance of the social demand for the use of a toolkit that ensures an in-depth study of social values, to date the methodological foundation, as a basis for the necessary empirical research, remains insufficiently formed, which requires turning to the theoretical conceptualization of the study of values.

It is important to note that modern science has developed a considerable number of theoretical and applied approaches to the study and assessment of values, which predominantly emphasize determining the position of each value in the hierarchy relative to one another, as, for example, is proposed in the well-known concepts of the empirical study of values by M. Rokeach [2], R. Inglehart and C. Welzel [3; 4], and others, whereas we are interested in assessing the level of formation of each value. This is one reason why it is also not possible to assess the values we study through the prism of the value continuum of S. Schwartz [5; 6], which is successfully used in a number of domestic studies conducted according to his methodology [7–9]. However, first of all, let us not forget that most empirical researchers of values, including those mentioned above, work with characteristics that simultaneously determine the ideality and the significance of various elements of life for a single individual; therefore, in this context we are speaking of the values of the personality — values based on the unique life experience of the individual — whereas traditional Russian values, in our view, are social values. In the terms of W. Thomas and F. Znaniecki, social values are "an initial fact that has an empirical content accessible to the members of a certain social group, and a meaning with respect to which it is or may be an object of activity" [10, p. 21]; that is, these are values conditioned by inclusion in social activity or in its result.

Thus, this article attempts to conceptualize the notion of social value and to develop a methodology for its sociological diagnosis using the example of traditional Russian values, as well as to describe the results of testing this methodology.

Since the author's empirical model is based on the idea of the need to use a set of indicators for a cumulative assessment of the perception of values, in the course of elaborating this approach we relied on the ideas of V. A. Yadov, which underlie his dispositional concept of the structure of the personality [11] and assume that a social subject possesses a complex system of various dispositional formations that regulate their behavior and activity. At the same time, we attempted to move away from the hierarchization of the elements under study, as well as from the originally psychological idea of D. N. Uznadze — underlying V. A. Yadov's concept — of the formation of attitudes against the background of unsatisfied needs.

Let us consider in detail the categories of the analysis of social values that we propose for conducting a sociological diagnosis of traditional Russian values.

## Theoretical Foundations

### Meaning-Defining Categories of the Analysis of Social Value

For a long time, the concept of value was synonymous with such universal human meanings as good, happiness, truth, honor, etc. This understanding of value has been preserved in everyday life to this day. Many understand value precisely as "socially approved ideas, shared by the majority of people, about what constitutes good, justice, patriotism, romantic love, friendship, and the like" [12, p. 306]. In the course of studying the scholarly literature reflecting various views and approaches to elucidating the concept of values, the author identified several key meaning-defining categories for their analysis, on which we will dwell in more detail.

The first category is "the ideal," which initially, in dialectical philosophy, is associated primarily with the world of ideas or the world of thoughts, and subsequently, in sociology, with respect to values, is used by M. Scheler and É. Durkheim in diametrically opposed senses.

Thus, É. Durkheim holds that values must be considered through ideals, for behind ideals there always stands a collective, social principle [13, p. 300], whereas M. Scheler sees in values a certain material a priori, which he defines as "all those ideal unities of meaning and all those states of affairs that become self-given in the content of immediate intuition, on condition of refraining from all assertions about the subjects thinking them, about the real natural properties of these subjects, and also on condition of refraining from all assertions about the object to which they might be applied" [14, p. 266]. In essence, M. Scheler's idealistic understanding of value comes down to a special kind of essence that takes place in a value-tinged state of affairs, which, in our view, is more akin to a mystical contemplation of reality and is closer to the doctrine of essences than to the sociology of values, and therefore does not reflect our views.

É. Durkheim substantively understands ideals as "simply ideas in which social life is depicted and generalized as it exists at the culminating points of its development" [13, p. 300], and functionally — as a certain regulator of the value of any thing, for it can change in accordance with a change in the ideal. Therefore, according to Durkheim, value, on the one hand, must "express the realities to which they are applied," and, on the other, "transform the realities to which they relate." And if in the first case it is a matter only of stating the property and content of a thing, "a concept in the proper sense," then in the second case what is meant is certain "value ideals" by which one must be guided in activity [13, p. 303] — that is, in essence, value ideals and goals declared and supported by social institutions, formed in the course of the historical development of society; they are prospective, developmental, a kind of referent for orientation in social reality.

From this we shall formulate the second category of the analysis of values, according to É. Durkheim, connected with the category of "the ideal" — the category of "the real." The difference between the ideal and the real lies in the fact that the ideal represents a certain idea that can serve as a goal toward which we strive, whereas the real represents the actual state of things.

The indicators describing the category of the ideal lie in the plane of the respondents' ideas about "the best in general"; these are certain abstract statements about the most important features of a specific social phenomenon, characterizing a conscious understanding of one's own position in the space of values, which determines a person's general value relation to the world; this is the respondents' general understanding of what is ideally acceptable for society with respect to a specific value.

The difference between the value indicators of "the ideal" and "the real" can be seen when analyzing such a traditional value as health. Here the formulation of "the ideal" may sound, for example, like the assertion of the thesis: "It would be good for any person to possess robust health," whereas "the real" represents the degree of agreement or disagreement with those actions that a person actually undertakes in order to preserve this health: "In case of malaise (illness) I usually consult doctors"; "I try to maintain a balance of work and rest"; "I try to eat rationally," and so on.

The third category of the measurement of values is "the significant." The concept of values, having originally philosophical roots as meaning-forming foundations of human existence, received its further interpretive development in sociology thanks to the representatives of the Baden School, W. Windelband and H. Rickert, who laid a peculiar bridge between philosophy and sociology.

H. Rickert supposes the essence of values to lie "in their significance, and not in their facticity" [15, p. 69–101]; for him, values represent neither a physical nor a psychical reality, yet they are nevertheless connected with it through culture and through progress.

M. Weber, continuing the tradition of H. Rickert, is the first to apply in sociology a special approach to the interpretation of the concept of value based on the Neo-Kantian approach, which excludes the positivist identification of values with goods. M. Weber, like H. Rickert, elucidates the concept of value through the definition of culture, considering it a value concept; however, he has the opposite understanding of the conceptual correlation of "value" and "significance." Accordingly, for the individual, only that part of reality is significant which is correlated with his value ideas that determine his individual interests [16, p. 374].

Here M. Weber, like H. Rickert, resorts to the use of "significance" for the conceptual comprehension of value, but in its mirror opposite: for H. Rickert, that which is significant possesses value; for Weber, on the contrary, that which possesses a certain value is significant. This dualistic understanding by sociologists of the essence of value is of unconditional interest to us. For, according to H. Rickert, there exists a cause-and-effect relationship between value and significance, according to which value is determined by the causality of its significance, which can be reflected in the formulation: "something is valuable to me because it is significant." For M. Weber, on the other hand, meaning is qualitatively identified through value, which sounds as follows: "that which is valuable is significant to me."

Let us note that H. Rickert [15, p. 69–101], and somewhat earlier W. Windelband [17; 18] as well, assumes that values are not objective properties of things; they are significant for a person as certain cognitive, aesthetic, and ethical principles. In other words: values organize all possible kinds of the subject's relation to reality. It is precisely on the basis of value premises that the social subject endows the objects of the material and spiritual world with a significance defined for himself, in accordance with which he realizes a certain behavioral strategy.

In this regard, in the context of this article we shall nevertheless settle on H. Rickert's position that it is precisely value that is determined by significance, and not the other way around.

Close in meaning to the category "the significant," but still somewhat different from it, is the fourth category: "the desirable."

According to the position of the contemporary Yugoslav sociologist Dragomir Pantić, values are "desirable characteristics" of an individual or group that "direct the behavior of their bearers toward certain goals" [19, p. 646–647]. In such a formulation, the category "the desirable" is identical to our understanding of the category "the significant"; however, these categories still substantially diverge, which is demonstrated quite clearly by the position of T. Parsons.

Thus, within the framework of T. Parsons's conceptions of the social system, values represent "conceptions of a desirable type of social system that regulate the processes by which acting subjects assume certain commitments" [20, p. 17–21]. The social system, in T. Parsons's opinion, includes roles and institutions that help society to function and to achieve its goals. These roles and institutions are based on values that are recognized by the given social system. For example, the value of the family is one of the fundamental traditional values of Russian society, and the social system — including laws, education, economic institutions, etc. — is created in such a way as to support and promote this value.

Since values are a set of principles that act as regulators of actions and are realized through the assumption of commitments within a system of the desirable type, the dialectical connection of the categories "the significant" and "the desirable" is evident, for they are connected as cause and effect: something is desirable for the individual because it is significant.

Let us note that every social system first exists potentially, and then may become actuality. It follows from this that the characteristic of value is its "desirability," and the indicator is "significance," which corresponds to the category of dialectical philosophy "the possible," describing, for example, the potential for the performance of a certain action in the future — that is, something not yet actual, but desirable because it is significant.

Thus, we speak of such a characteristic as "the desirable" and therefore "the possible," meaning certain preferences that are formed on the basis of socio-psychological inclinations as a result of justifying the choice between possible decisions. This "desirable" is measured by measuring "significance" — the systemic meaning (sense) of an object, that is, the meaning that an object acquires by being included in a certain social context or in a social system. For example, if it is a matter of the value of robust health, then its desirability is determined through the analysis of scaled statements about how significant it is for the respondent "to have robust health"; if we speak of the value of creative labor and collectivism, then we use the analysis of the respondents' assessments of the significance of "Having the opportunity to work toward the achievement of collective goals, to create something new."

Speaking of "the desirable" as relatively possible — based on the fact that some event may occur because it is significant, or may not occur because the individual overestimates the significance of the indicator for himself and in reality may not perform a single corresponding action — it is necessary to speak also of absolute possibility, which presupposes an action closer to reality, and which can be expressed by the fifth category of the definition of values — "the expected," measured through an assessment of the degree of "readiness" to perform this or that action within the framework of a shared value. In the terms formulated by the Polish scholar F. Znaniecki and the American scholar W. Thomas [10, p. 22], this is undoubtedly a matter of a social attitude.

According to the concept of W. Thomas and F. Znaniecki, the social attitude is the individual analogue of the social value, and activity, in whatever form, is the connecting link between them. In essence, a social attitude is the individual's readiness to display a certain type of behavior or reaction in a social situation, which is formed under the influence of values.

One cannot investigate social values without social attitudes, nor social attitudes without social values. In the context of constructing an empirical toolkit for the sociological diagnosis of values, in order to assess value judgments within the category of "the expected," we use the indicator "readiness" to perform this or that action within the framework of a shared value. For example, if it is a matter of the value of robust health, then the "expectedness" of an action can be assessed by means of the statement: "I am ready to lead a healthy lifestyle in order to have robust health."

As a result of the analysis conducted of approaches to conceptualizing the notion of value, four categories emerged, together with four indicative characteristics corresponding to each of them, which jointly describe the empirical model of the analysis of social value (Table 1).

The empirical model of the analysis of social value described forms the methodological basis for sociological diagnosis, defining its stages, and includes:

– an assessment of the ideal value relation to social reality within the framework of each value studied;
– an assessment of the significance of each indicator included in the description of a specific value;
– an assessment of readiness to perform actions corresponding to each value studied;
– an assessment of the reflection of adherence to a value in specific actions and deeds (action);
– the correlation of the indicated assessments and the determination of the gaps between real actions and the ideal level of the significance of values.

On the basis of the elements of value analysis presented above, it can be concluded that **social values are certain temporally stable, socially significant universals that: determine a person's relation to social reality; characterize the potential readiness to follow these universals; include a set of actions reflecting adherence to these universals; and act as individual or group reference points of social activity, simultaneously prompting it and serving as its social regulators** [21].

**Table 1 – An Empirical Model of Social Value Analysis**

| Category | Indicative characteristic / feature | Manifestation |
|---|---|---|
| The ideal | Value relation to social reality | An idea of the best in general;<br>- an abstract statement about the most important features of any social phenomenon;<br>- statement: "that which is ideal for society" |
| The desirable (the possible) | Value preference (significance) | Preferences based on inclinations (the justification of the choice between possible decisions);<br>- what is significant in connection with building a career;<br>- statement: "we want"; "it is significant for us" |
| The expected (the probable) | Value attitude (readiness) | Concrete intentions; social attitudes;<br>– readiness to react to the object of the relation;<br>- what a person is ready to do for the sake of a specific value;<br>- statement: "I am ready" |
| The real (the actual) | Value action | The actual manifestation of the relation to a value in action;<br>- what a person is doing now "within the framework" of a specific value;<br>- statement: "It exists in fact" |

## Results

### Testing the Methodology of Value Assessment

In order to test the author's methodology of the sociological diagnosis of social values using the example of traditional Russian values, from May 17 to June 1, 2023, the author conducted an online sociological study, "Traditional and Strategic Values of Student Youth" [22], among full-time RANEPA students (N=4338) through their personal accounts, of whom 89% are students enrolled in bachelor's degree programs and 11% are students enrolled in master's degree programs, evenly distributed across years of study and regions of study; 64.5% are female and 35.5% male — in proportion to the gender structure of students at RANEPA.

It is important to note that the object of our study was representatives of student youth as a socio-demographic group that constitutes the potential for the socio-economic growth and development of Russian society, and that is also in the focus of the formation of traditional values, being included in the educational process, which is increasingly integrating value-oriented activities into its program.

The diagnostic toolkit that became the basis for this study structurally corresponds to the structure of the analysis of social values described by us earlier. As for the list of values diagnosed, it differs somewhat from the original list given in Decree of the President of the Russian Federation No. 809, owing to the specific nature of the sociological approach to analyzing each value separately.

Based on the survey results, the assessments of each element of a value were correlated with one another, and the results were arranged in a hierarchical order according to the level of significance for the respondents, thanks to which it is clearly visible which values are fully shared and which are shared only partially (Table 2). The final assignment of a particular value to a specific assessment group was determined by the maximum share of respondents who gave the maximum rating to each of its indicators; if, for example, among the four indicators three fall into the first group and one into the fourth, then the value falls into the fourth group.

## Discussion

Thanks to the data presented in Table 2, one can see a noticeable unevenness in the respondents' adherence to the values studied, which required the development of an additional instrument to ensure uniformity in the description of the results and clarity in their presentation: a scale was used, constructed on the basis of the proportional correspondence between the share of the ratings of each statement and the value of an ordinal scale.

As a result, the following groups of values were obtained:

- the **first group** — "fully formed values" (81–100% of respondents): the value of human rights and freedoms;
- the **second group** — "rather formed values" (61–80% of respondents): spiritual and moral values (humanism, justice, dignity, responsibility) and values characterizing the attitude toward the country (patriotism, service to the Fatherland and responsibility for its fate, continuity of generations, unity of the peoples of Russia);
- the **third group** — "half-formed values" (41–60% of respondents): values based predominantly on characteristics of a person's attitude toward themselves and on features of social interaction (health, family, mutual assistance, historical memory);
- the **fourth group** — "rather unformed values" (21–40% of respondents): values that, on the whole, characterize a person's attitude toward those around them and toward labor (life, mercy, mutual respect, creative labor, and collectivism);
- the **fifth group** — "completely unformed values" (less than 20%): no value.

Here it is important to note that in the course of this study we did not set ourselves the goal of characterizing the attitude toward traditional Russian values of the entire student youth of the country. It was important for us to test the developed methodology of value diagnosis; therefore, the sample certainly does not represent the entire student youth of Russia and characterizes only the students of one specific university.

## Conclusions

Undoubtedly, the values we study are, on the whole, not new for Russian society in general or for student youth in particular. However, many of them appeared in public discourse relatively recently, almost simultaneously with the appearance of the regulatory legal acts establishing them. Many of these values were either passed over in silence, or re-evaluated, or else devalued for many years, which was reflected in the level of their significance for the respondents; therefore, the value system of student youth is quite heterogeneous.

First of all, let us turn our attention to the values whose formation is targeted by a whole series of activities organized by actors at various levels of youth human-resource policy; these fall into the first two assessment groups, being fully or almost fully shared by the majority of respondents. At the same time, there stand out values, some of which came into the sphere of attention of the general public comparatively recently, with the adoption of the regulatory legal acts containing them. However, the focus of attention on these values is still not concentrated among most actors of youth policy, which is reflected in their position in the value structure of the respondents. The exception is, perhaps, only two values: the value of health and the value of the family, which for quite a long time have been key to a whole range of both national projects and federal programs, yet, as of the moment of conducting this study, are not fully formed within the student youth milieu.

On the basis of the results of the sociological diagnosis of values conducted using the methodological approach described in this article, it can be said that the currently established structure of the values of student youth is not entirely stable, since practically all the elements of the values comprising it are at different levels of formation.

In addition, a serious gap is evident between the ideal, abstract understanding of values and the real, actual actions that ought to be performed in following specific values. Hence, one cannot fail to note the need to develop social technologies for working with each value separately on the basis of their analysis in terms of the assessments of each component: value relations, value preferences, value attitudes, value actions.
